Your Optimizer Is (Approximately) Doing Fisher Flow

March 14, 2026 6 min read
fisher-information natural-gradient optimization information-geometry

Adam’s second moment estimate tracks a diagonal approximation to the empirical Fisher Information Matrix. K-FAC uses a Kronecker-factored approximation. EWC uses the Fisher as a regularization penalty. Natural gradient uses the full thing. They’re all doing the same computation at different fidelity levels.

None of this is my observation. Amari formalized natural gradient in 1998. Khan and Rue (2023) unified these algorithms (and more) under a single Bayesian learning rule. Martens (2020) wrote a 76-page treatment that covers most of the relevant theory. Kunstner, Hennig, and Balles (2019) showed where the empirical Fisher approximation breaks down.

I built a small package (fisherflow) that makes these connections executable. You can run both sides of each equivalence and watch the parameter trajectories match (or not). This post is a walkthrough.

Fisher Information in 30 Seconds

Start with a parametric model $p(x | \theta)$, where $x$ is the observed data and $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the parameter vector you’re estimating. The score is the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to the parameters:

$$s(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \log p(x|\theta)$$

The Fisher Information Matrix $\mathcal{I}(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the expected outer product of the score:

$$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x|\theta)}\left[s(\theta) \, s(\theta)^\top\right]$$

where the expectation is over data drawn from the model. Equivalently, $\mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}[\nabla^2_\theta \log p(x|\theta)]$, the expected negative Hessian. It measures how sharply the likelihood peaks around the current parameters. Sharper peak, more information, less uncertainty.

The property that makes everything work: Fisher information from independent observations $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ adds up.

$$\mathcal{I}_{1:n}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathcal{I}_{x_i}(\theta)$$

where $\mathcal{I}_{x_i}(\theta)$ is the Fisher contribution from observation $x_i$.

So sequential estimation is bookkeeping. Maintain a running estimate $\hat\theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (your best guess at time $t$) and accumulated information $\mathcal{I}t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (your running total of Fisher information). When new data arrives with score $s_t$ and Fisher contribution $\mathcal{I}{\text{new}}$:

$$\mathcal{I}_t = \mathcal{I}_{t-1} + \mathcal{I}_{\text{new}}$$

$$\hat\theta_t = \hat\theta_{t-1} + \mathcal{I}_t^{-1} \cdot s_t$$

The $\mathcal{I}_t^{-1}$ preconditioner is what makes this a natural gradient. It rescales the update to respect the geometry of the parameter space (the Fisher-Rao metric on the statistical manifold) rather than treating all directions equally.

The Optimizer Taxonomy

Different optimizers approximate the Fisher at different levels:

MethodFisher approximationCostWhat’s lost
SGD$\mathcal{I} = \lambda I_d$ (scalar times identity)$O(1)$All curvature information
Adam$\mathcal{I} = \text{diag}(v)$ (diagonal, $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$)$O(d)$Off-diagonal interactions
K-FAC$\mathcal{I} \approx A \otimes B$ ($A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ for a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$)$O(m^3 + n^3)$Cross-layer interactions
Natural gradientFull $\mathcal{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$$O(d^3)$Nothing (but intractable for large $d$)

Here $d$ is the total number of parameters, $I_d$ is the $d \times d$ identity matrix, $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product, and $A$, $B$ are the input covariance and output gradient covariance factors from K-FAC (Martens and Grosse, 2015).

The package lets you run both sides and verify each one. The Adam case is the most interesting because it’s the most widely used and the equivalence is the most approximate.

Adam Is Simplified Diagonal Fisher Flow

Adam’s second moment $\hat{v}t = \beta_2 \hat{v}{t-1} + (1-\beta_2) g_t^2$ (where $g_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the gradient at step $t$, $\beta_2 \in [0,1)$ is the decay rate, and the squaring is element-wise) tracks the diagonal of an exponentially-weighted empirical Fisher. The equivalence isn’t exact. It requires simplifications:

  1. No bias correction (remove the $1/(1 - \beta_2^t)$ term)
  2. No epsilon
  3. Use $v_t$ directly instead of $\sqrt{v_t}$ (this one matters: Adam uses a square-root preconditioner, not the standard natural gradient preconditioner)
  4. The “Fisher” Adam estimates is the empirical Fisher (outer product of gradients at current parameters), not the true Fisher (expectation under the model’s own distribution). These differ away from convergence (Kunstner et al., 2019).

The fisherflow package maintains two parallel parameter trajectories and checks they match to machine precision:

from fisherflow import AdamAsFisherFlow
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
conn = AdamAsFisherFlow(d=5, lr=0.01, beta2=0.999)

for step in range(50):
    grad = np.random.randn(5)
    conn.verify_equivalence(grad)  # asserts match to 1e-12

print("All 50 steps matched.")

Full Adam (with sqrt, bias correction, epsilon) diverges. You can measure the gap:

conn_full = AdamAsFisherFlow(d=3, lr=0.01, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, eps=1e-8)
conn_ff = AdamAsFisherFlow(d=3, lr=0.01, beta2=0.999)

grad = np.random.randn(3)
theta_adam = conn_full.step_adam_full(grad)
theta_ff = conn_ff.step_fisher_flow(grad)

print(f"Gap: {np.linalg.norm(theta_adam - theta_ff):.6f}")
# Non-zero — the simplifications matter.

He (2024) provides a rigorous treatment in the FAdam paper.

EWC Is Fisher Regularization by Definition

EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) uses the Fisher directly in its loss function. There’s no approximation to demonstrate because EWC was defined this way:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{new}}(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{d} F_i (\theta_i - \theta_i^*)^2$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{new}}$ is the loss on the new task, $\lambda > 0$ controls regularization strength, $F_i$ is the $i$-th diagonal entry of the Fisher from the previous task, $\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are the parameters after training on the previous task, and the sum runs over all $d$ parameters. Parameters with high $F_i$ were important for the old task and get penalized for moving.

Verifying the identity is exact:

from fisherflow import EWCAsFisherFlow

conn = EWCAsFisherFlow(d=4, lambda_ewc=1.0)

# Train on task 1
for _ in range(20):
    conn.step_task(np.random.randn(4))
conn.consolidate()

# EWC penalty === Fisher regularization (by construction)
theta_new = conn.theta + 0.1 * np.random.randn(4)
assert conn.ewc_penalty(theta_new) == conn.fisher_regularization(theta_new)

When the Theory Applies (and When It Doesn’t)

Two regimes.

Classical. You train to convergence. The Fisher at the optimum is the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood. Asymptotic theory applies: consistency, normality, Cramer-Rao efficiency. Fisher-based uncertainty quantification is meaningful.

Modern ML. You stop before convergence. The Fisher accumulated along the training trajectory is not the Fisher at any optimum. It encodes something about the path through parameter space, not about curvature at a stationary point.

Most of the elegant theory (additivity, efficiency, $\mathcal{I}^{-1}$ as uncertainty) assumes you’re in the classical regime. In practice, you’re usually not. Barrett and Dherin (2021) showed that SGD implicitly penalizes the trace of the empirical Fisher. Jastrzebski et al. (2021) showed that the Fisher trace early in training critically affects generalization. The Fisher still matters in the modern regime. It just means something different.

The package is at github.com/queelius/fisher-flow, with 5 tutorial notebooks that cover everything here with live code.
